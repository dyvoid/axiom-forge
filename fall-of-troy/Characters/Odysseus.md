## Description & History
King of Ithaca, Odysseus was not the strongest Greek at Troy, nor the fastest, nor the most fearsome in open battle. He was something rarer and more dangerous: the cleverest. Where Achilles won by force and Agamemnon by authority, Odysseus won by thinking three moves ahead of everyone in the room.

He was reportedly reluctant to join the war, feigning madness to avoid the call. When Palamedes tested him by placing his infant son in the path of his plough, Odysseus stopped — and from that moment was bound to the campaign. He became Agamemnon's most trusted counsellor, the man sent to negotiate impossible situations, to retrieve Achilles from his tent, to steal the Palladium from within Troy's walls, and to maintain the fragile coalition of Greek kings across a decade of frustration and failure.

The Trojan Horse was his idea. It was, by any measure, the most consequential act of deception in the ancient world — a single stroke that ended a ten-year war that no amount of force had managed to conclude. He conceived it, built the case for it, and climbed inside it himself.
